Aim. Write the training loop that turns the random model into one that has learned something, and see how each language finds the gradients (torch does it for you; the others do it by hand).

The loop

Training repeats five moves for every batch:

1. Zeroforget the old gradients
→
2. Forwardrun the model, measure the loss
→
3. Backwardhow should every weight change to lower the loss?
→
4. Updatenudge every weight a little (AdamW)
→
5. Reportnow and then, show the loss and a sample
for each epoch:
    for each batch of the training data:
        zero the gradients
        loss = cross_entropy(model(inputs), targets)
        backward pass  -> a gradient for every weight
        optimizer step -> every weight moves a little
        every few steps: print training and validation loss

An epoch is one pass over all training batches. We run 2 epochs of 72 batches: 144 steps.

  • The loss starts at the uniform-guess level, 10.8, and in 144 steps drops to about 6. The model has learned which tokens are common (commas, periods, "the") and puts most of its probability there.
  • That is also what its text shows: after epoch 1 it repeats the most common token, a comma. This matches what the book sees at the start of its much longer training.
  • The validation loss stays above the training loss: the model does better on text it trains on. With more steps and a bigger model this gap becomes overfitting: the book's model ends at training loss 0.4 and validation loss 6.4, having memorised the story.
  • Our model has 3 million parameters and sees 9,000 tokens. It cannot learn grammar in that. It shows the mechanics; the real model comes from OpenAI's weights in 5.5.

Step 3 in torch, and in the other four languages

The backward pass computes the gradient of the loss for every weight: if I make this weight a little bigger, does the loss go up or down, and how fast? The gradient is what the optimizer needs.

Python

loss.backward()
torch's autograd recorded every operation of the forward pass and replays it backwards.

Go, Rust, C, Zig

No autograd. grad.* runs the forward pass again while remembering what every layer saw, then walks the layers backwards applying the chain rule. About 200 lines per language; the rules are on the extra page.
How do we know the hand-written backward pass is right? Three checks. (1) On the same model and batch, the gradient of every tensor matches torch's autograd (sums of absolute values agree to 5 digits; the Rust test pins them). (2) A finite-difference test in Go nudges a weight and compares the change of the loss with the gradient. (3) The first 30 training losses of Rust and torch agree to about 1e-6.

The optimizer: AdamW

Plain gradient descent moves every weight by learning_rate x gradient. AdamW is smarter: for each weight it keeps two running averages, the average gradient (which way to go) and the average squared gradient (how noisy it is), and divides one by the other. Weights with steady gradients move at a steady pace; noisy ones move carefully. It also shrinks every weight a tiny bit each step (weight decay) to keep them small. The book uses a learning rate of 0.0004 for its big model; our tiny one uses 0.003. Weight decay is 0.1 in both. The arithmetic is exactly torch.optim.AdamW; a worked example shows it for one weight.

Same answer in four languages. Go, Rust, C and Zig print identical numbers, down to the bytes of the saved model (5.4). Python (torch) agrees to three decimals for the first ~100 steps and then drifts slightly: torch adds up the numbers of a matrix product in a different order, and training amplifies such tiny differences. Both are right.

Exercises

  1. Train for 4 epochs instead of 2 (EPOCHS in the demo). What do the training and validation losses do?
  2. Set the learning rate to 0.03 and then to 0.00003. What happens, and why?
  3. Why does the loop call zero_grads before every step?
Answers1) The training loss keeps falling while the validation loss stalls or rises: the model starts to memorise. 2) Too large: the loss jumps around or blows up; too small: it barely moves in 144 steps. 3) Gradients are added into their buffers; without zeroing, each step would include the gradients of all earlier steps.
