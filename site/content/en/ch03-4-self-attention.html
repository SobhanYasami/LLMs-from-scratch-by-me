Aim. Add three weight matrices so the model can learn what to look for, and see why scores are divided by the square root of the key dimension.

This is the mechanism inside the original transformer and every GPT, called scaled dot-product attention. The structure is the same as 3.3. What changes: the vectors we compare are no longer the raw embeddings but projections of them.

Why not just keep using x?

In 3.3 one vector xi did three jobs: it was the thing asking, the thing compared against, and the thing mixed in. That is too rigid. The word "it" should look for a noun, but it is not a noun that other words want to copy. Three different matrices let the model give each token three different "faces".

A library. You hold a note saying what you want (the query). Every book has a label on its spine (the key) and contents inside (the value). You compare your note with the spine labels, decide how much each book matters, then read a blend of the contents. A spine label does not have to equal the contents, and that is exactly why keys and values are separate.

Query, key, value

RoleComputed asThink of it as
query qix @ W_query"what am I looking for?" (a search query)
key kix @ W_key"what do I offer to searches?" (an index)
value vix @ W_value"what do I hand over if chosen?" (the stored content)

The names come from databases: you match a query against keys, then read the matching values. Here the model learns W_query, W_key and W_value during training. Every token is projected three times, once by each matrix.

What did the new weights change?

Here is "journey" before and after. The grey dashed bars are the weights of 3.3 (raw embeddings). The blue bars use the projections above.

The change is small because these matrices are random numbers from a seed, not trained yet. The point of this page is the machinery: now the weights are parameters, so gradient descent can adjust them. One visible difference already: scores are no longer symmetric. ω23 = 1.8111 but ω32 = 1.8284, because the query of one token is compared with the key of the other, and those come from different matrices.

A toy example: why a learned query matters

Take the sentence "The cat sat on the mat because it was tired". To understand "it" we must find what "it" refers to. Give each token two made-up features, [animal-ness, object-ness]: cat = [1.0, 0.2], mat = [0.1, 1.0], it = [0.5, 0.5]. These numbers are invented for illustration, not taken from a trained model.

Why divide by sqrt(d_k)?

Dot products grow with the number of dimensions. Large scores push softmax toward a hard "one winner" distribution, where gradients nearly vanish and learning stalls. Dividing by the square root of the key dimension keeps the scores in a range where softmax stays soft. The extra on scaling shows real numbers.

Do not confuse weight parameters (W_query, W_key, W_value: learned, fixed after training) with attention weights (the softmax output: computed fresh for every input).

Code

The book's two classes

The book codes this as SelfAttention_v1 (raw nn.Parameter(torch.rand(...))) and SelfAttention_v2 (nn.Linear without bias). They compute the same thing; v2 only has a better weight initialisation, which is why they print different numbers from different seeds. Our SelfAttention is v1-style with weights passed in, so every language can use identical numbers.

Why can the score of "journey" for "starts" differ from the score of "starts" for "journey"?The first compares the query of "journey" with the key of "starts"; the second compares the query of "starts" with the key of "journey".
